    Slapback Delay

    What it is

    A single, fast echo — one repeat, no feedback, about 75–150 ms behind the original. Too quick to hear as a separate echo, so it thickens a sound into a doubling instead.

    It's the sound of 1950s rock 'n' roll — Sam Phillips put it on Elvis at Sun Studios in 1954 — and the exam keeps asking how you'd make it, decade by decade.

    01 — How it's made

    One repeat, loud, and fast enough to fool the ear.

    Any delay works by holding a copy of the sound and playing it back a moment later. Slapback is the simplest possible setting: the copy comes back once, and feedback is off, so it never repeats again.

    The trick is the timing. At roughly 75–150 ms the single repeat lands too soon for your ear to register it as a separate event, so your brain fuses the original and the repeat into one bigger sound. That's why slapback fattens a vocal or guitar — it doubles it — rather than echoing it.

    Push the delay much shorter and the two collapse into a hollow, comb-filtered tone; stretch it past about 150 ms and the repeat pulls away into an audible slap you hear on its own. Slapback lives in the narrow band in between.

    Keep the repeat loud (a high wet level) and you get the classic effect: a doubling that adds size and urgency without smearing the words.

    + The why — the maths (optional)

    Slapback was born on tape, and the delay time was a physical distance. A tape machine writes the sound with a record head, then reads it back a moment later with a separate playback head. The gap between those heads, divided by how fast the tape moves, is the delay:

    delay time = head gap ÷ tape speed

    Close the heads up, or run the tape faster, and the echo shortens; widen the gap, or slow the tape, and it lengthens. Slapback is just this set short, with the feedback path switched off so the sound passes the playback head exactly once.

    The numbers are real. At Sun the tape ran at 15 ips (inches per second), and the head gap worked out to roughly 125 ms — the classic Memphis slapback. Halve the speed to 7½ ips and the same gap doubles the delay to about 250 ms. With no varispeed, those two tape speeds were effectively your only two slapback times.

    It also explains the warmth. Tape behaves like a gentle low-pass filter, so the repeat returns a shade darker than the original; wow & flutter (tiny slow and fast speed variations) add a faint pitch wobble; and pushed hard, the tape saturates, adding warm harmonics. A digital delay has none of this — its repeat is a clean, identical copy. That darker repeat is your single best clue the slapback is tape, not digital.

    And the reason the timing window matters is perceptual, not mechanical:

    under ~40 mstoo short to separate — the copy fuses tonally into the original (the Haas / precedence effect), heard as comb-filtering / flanging colour, not a doubling. With feedback it rings at a pitch of about 1000 ÷ delay-in-ms Hz.
    ~75–150 msslapback — one quick repeat the ear can't separate as an event, so it reads as a thicker, doubled sound.
    over ~150–200 msthe repeat separates into a distinct echo; add feedback and it becomes a repeating delay.

    the slapback fingerprint · level over time

    One loud original, one repeat — then silence. An echo would keep going (the faint dashes); slapback stops at one.

    04 — In the exam

    Identify it, then place it and judge it.

    The 2024 paper (Q6, "Atlantic") asked candidates to evaluate the use of delay and explain how delay was made from the 1940s to today — so the era lineage above isn't decoration, it is the second half of the answer. Name the effect, then walk the decades.

    AO3 · identify

    What you hear → what you name

    A tight, single, fast repeat that thickens a vocal or guitar — one slap, no repeating tail, no wash. Name it slapback delay.

    The repeat's tone dates it: a warm, slightly darker repeat is tape; a clean, identical one is digital or a plug-in. The darkening is your cleanest tape-vs-digital cue.

    • One repeat — not a train of echoes (that's feedback)
    • Short — it fattens, it doesn't echo
    • Level varies — loud and obvious (Elvis), or low and subtle (Toto, "Africa")
    AO4 · place & judge

    Why it's used, and where it sits in time

    The marks come from justifying it: slapback is most widely used on vocals, giving a doubling / layered effect without affecting clarity — size and energy without mud.

    Then walk the decades: tape (1950s) → dedicated tape echo (Copicat, Space Echo, Echoplex, Echorec) → bucket-brigade pedals (1970s) → digital (late 1970s) → plug-in (1990s+).

    • ≈ 80–150 ms, single repeat, no feedback, high wet level
    • The defining sound of 1950s rock 'n' roll (Elvis, Sun Studios)

    05 — Retrieve

    Cover the answers. Tap to check.

    What three things define slapback delay?
    One repeat (no feedback), a short delay of roughly 75–150 ms, and a loud wet level — heard as a doubling, not a distinct echo.

    Which studio, producer and record launched slapback?
    Sun Studios; Sam Phillips; Elvis Presley, "That's All Right" (1954). It became the defining sound of 1950s rock 'n' roll.

    Why does slapback fatten a sound instead of echoing it?
    At ~75–150 ms the single repeat is too fast to hear as a separate event, so the ear fuses original + repeat into one larger, doubled sound. Past ~150 ms it separates into an audible echo.

    Trace the devices that made slapback/echo from the 1950s on.
    Tape machine (1954) → dedicated tape echo (Watkins Copicat, 1957) → multi-head tape echo (Roland RE-201, 1973) → bucket-brigade pedals (1970s) → digital delay (late 1970s) → plug-in (1990s+).
    tap to reveal
    Why does a tape slapback sound warmer than a digital one?
    Tape acts as a gentle low-pass filter, so each repeat is darker; wow & flutter add a pitch wobble and saturation adds warm harmonics. A digital delay returns a clean, identical copy — the darkening is the giveaway.
    tap to reveal
    How is slapback different from ADT?
    Both are short delays, but ADT (≈15–40 ms) sits in the Haas zone and adds pitch modulation, so the ear fuses it into one wider voice. Slapback (≈75–150 ms) is longer, unmodulated, and heard as a separate quick repeat.
